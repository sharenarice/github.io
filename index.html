I am Head of Neuroscience Clinical Trials and Partnerships at Radial, the largest brain medicine clinical network in the United States.

I earned my PhD in neuroscience at the University of Michigan, where I worked on the discovery and characterization of a brain rhythm the two hemispheres trade in opposite phase. As the first full-time hire at a focused ultrasound company, I then worked to bring a device from concept to multi-site clinical trials.

My path runs through discovery research, device testing, quality engineering, venture diligence, journalism, and advising, so I read the field from more than one seat. I turn what each seat learns into free tools and references others can use.

As a child, I wrote a 45 page typed documentary on human behavior because I was curious. In graduate school, I lived and trained at the Ann Arbor Zen Buddhist Temple for 22 months. You cannot meditate your way through the FDA, but you can learn to observe lines of thought.

Field Potentials

A Systems Account of Clinical Neuro

Clinical neuro keeps its promises more often when the people in it can see past their own seat. Most of the field’s failures happen at the seams, where a researcher, a regulator, a payer, a clinician, and a builder each hold one piece and no one is paid to watch the joins.

Field Potentials maps those seams. It is written for anyone who works in clinical neuro, drugs and devices alike, or lives with what it produces, and it assumes little beyond curiosity.

  • 1212 seats, from trainee and discovery researcher to payer and person with lived experience, with who pays for each
  • 1515 words that mean different things in different seats, set side by side in the Neurosetta Stone
  • 77 gates an intervention passes to reach a patient and stay in use
  • 2222 field diagnostic mechanisms, each with a key question to ask aloud and a first move from your seat

Field Potentials supersedes MECHANISMS, my earlier account of medical neurotechnology.

    Discovery research

    A brain rhythm the two hemispheres trade in opposite phase

    In my PhD research at the University of Michigan, I helped discover and characterize splines: a fast rhythm near 140 Hz in the retrosplenial cortex, a hub for navigation and memory. The left and right sides fire in opposite phase, like the interlocking teeth of gears, and the pattern grows more precise the faster an animal runs. It returns during REM sleep, when most dreaming happens.

    Cell Reports paper, co-first author (opens in a new tab) Dissertation (opens in a new tab)
