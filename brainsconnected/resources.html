Two shelves, one field

The same technology looks completely different depending on which side of it you're standing on.

If you wear these devices, you need to know what a number means and who else can see it. If you build them, you need signal processing, datasets, and a clear read on the law. Pick your shelf. Most people end up browsing both.

Before you buy anything

The single most useful thing we can give you isn't a product list, it's a set of questions that separates a real device from a well-photographed one. Print this, or just keep it open in a tab while you shop.

The eight-question buyer's checklist

  • What exactly does it measure, in units? If the answer is only a branded score with no underlying signal named, you can't compare it to anything else or check it against research.
  • Where does the data go, and can you export it? An export button is the clearest single signal that a company expects you to outlive your subscription.
  • Is the core function behind a subscription? Work out the two-year cost, not the sticker price. This reorders most shopping lists immediately.
  • What's the return window, and does opened count? Comfort is the number one reason devices get abandoned, and you cannot assess comfort in a store.
  • How long does setup take on a bad morning? Ten minutes of electrode fiddling is fine on day one and fatal by day twelve.
  • Is there a specific regulatory clearance for the specific claim? "Registered with" and "designed in accordance with" are not clearance. Cleared for one indication does not cover others.
  • What happens if the company shuts down? Ask whether the device keeps working offline. Plenty of hardware has been bricked by a server being switched off.
  • Who has reviewed it after ninety days? Launch reviews measure novelty. Only long-term users can tell you about strap wear, app rot, and whether it's still in a drawer.

Understanding what your device is telling you

Consumer neurotech generates a lot of numbers and very little explanation of them. These are the concepts that make the dashboard legible.

What EEG actually sees Explainer

Electrodes on the scalp read summed electrical activity from large populations of neurons, filtered through skull and skin. That means good sensitivity to rhythmic states and poor spatial precision. Understanding this one limitation makes most overclaiming obvious on sight.

The brainwave bands, without the mysticism Explainer

Delta, theta, alpha, beta, and gamma are frequency ranges, not moods. Alpha rises when you close your eyes. That's a robust, replicable finding, and it's also the entire basis for a surprising amount of marketing copy.

Artifacts: why your "focus" spiked when you chewed Explainer

Jaw clenches, blinks, and eye movements produce electrical signals far larger than cortical activity. Every EEG device fights these. Knowing what an artifact looks like is the fastest way to develop a healthy skepticism about your own data.

Heart rate variability, the useful cousin Explainer

HRV is cheaper to measure, better validated, and often more actionable than consumer EEG for stress and recovery. If you're buying your first device for regulation rather than curiosity, start here and save several hundred dollars.

Sleep staging and what "deep sleep" means Explainer

Staging comes from characteristic patterns in brain, eye, and muscle signals. Wrist-only devices infer stages from movement and heart rate, which is a genuinely harder problem and a much looser estimate than an EEG headband gives you.

Reading a study without a PhD Explainer

Four questions handle most of it: how many people, were they blinded, what was the control condition, and did the outcome measure exist before the data came in. Sample sizes under twenty and open-label designs deserve caution, not dismissal.

Your brain data and your rights

Neural data has started getting its own legal treatment, separate from ordinary health data. This is moving fast, and it varies enormously by where you live.

Where the law currently stands

Several US states have amended their privacy statutes to treat neural data as sensitive personal information, with Colorado and California among the first to do so in 2024. Internationally, Chile amended its constitution to protect mental integrity, and UNESCO adopted a global recommendation on neurotechnology ethics. None of this is settled, and the practical effect on a device you buy today is still limited.

Treat this as orientation rather than legal advice, and check your own jurisdiction. We'd rather tell you the map is incomplete than pretend it isn't.

Questions worth emailing a vendor before you buy

  • Is my raw neural data stored on your servers, or only on my device?
  • Do you sell, share, or license any derived data to third parties, including in aggregate or de-identified form?
  • Can I delete everything, and does deletion include backups and derived models?
  • Is my data used to train models, and can I opt out without losing features?
  • What happens to my data if you're acquired? Privacy policies frequently survive acquisitions less well than the data does.

A company that answers these clearly and quickly is telling you something important. So is one that doesn't answer at all.

When it isn't working

Bad signal quality Troubleshooting

Nine times out of ten it's contact. Hair between electrode and scalp, dry skin, or a strap that's loosened. Clean the contacts, wet the skin slightly if the device allows it, and sit still for the first thirty seconds while it settles.

Wild readings at the same time each day Troubleshooting

Look for electrical noise: chargers, laptop power supplies, fluorescent lights, and anything with a motor. Mains hum shows up at 50 or 60 hertz depending on your country and it is astonishingly good at ruining a recording.

The numbers don't match how you feel Troubleshooting

Sometimes the device is wrong. Sometimes you are. Keep a short subjective log alongside the data for two weeks before concluding either. If they never converge, the metric isn't measuring what you care about, and that's worth knowing.

It's in a drawer Troubleshooting

The most common outcome and the least discussed. Usually comfort, setup friction, or the absence of a decision the data could actually change. Post about it in the forums; abandonment stories are more useful to other buyers than reviews are.

Start here, spend nothing

You can get remarkably far before buying hardware. Public datasets and mature open-source tooling will teach you what these signals look like, and more importantly what they don't look like.

MNE-Python Open source

The most complete open toolkit for EEG and MEG analysis: filtering, artifact removal, epoching, source localization, and visualization. Excellent tutorials that double as a course in the underlying signal processing.

mne.tools

BrainFlow Open source

One API across a wide range of consumer and research biosensor boards. Worth adopting early: it means swapping hardware later doesn't mean rewriting your acquisition layer.

brainflow.org

Lab Streaming Layer Open source

The de facto standard for synchronizing multiple data streams in real time with sane timestamps. If your experiment involves a stimulus and a signal, you'll need this sooner than you think.

labstreaminglayer.org

OpenNeuro Datasets

A large public archive of neuroimaging and electrophysiology datasets, many organized in BIDS. Load someone else's clean EEG before you generate your own messy recording.

openneuro.org

PhysioNet Datasets

Long-running repository of physiological signal data with strong documentation, including EEG, ECG, and sleep recordings. Good source of well-annotated data for benchmarking a pipeline.

physionet.org

EEG-BIDS Standard

The Brain Imaging Data Structure extended to EEG. Adopting a standard layout early is the difference between a dataset others can use and a folder only you understand.

bids.neuroimaging.io

Hardware you can actually build on

The practical question isn't which headset is best, it's which one gives you raw data, a documented SDK, and a licence that permits what you intend to do.

Open-source EEG boards Hardware

Open hardware platforms give you full raw access, schematics, and no gatekeeping on what you build. More setup effort than a consumer headband, dramatically more freedom, and the community support is genuinely good.

Example: OpenBCI

Consumer headsets with developer APIs Commercial

Faster to a working demo and far more comfortable, with the tradeoff that raw data access, licensing tiers, and commercial terms vary a lot between vendors. Read the developer licence before you design around one.

EMG and neural input wristbands Commercial

Reading muscle activity at the forearm sidesteps the skull entirely and delivers vastly better bandwidth and latency than scalp EEG. If your application is control rather than state measurement, start here.

Rolling your own analog front end Hardware

Purpose-built biopotential amplifier chips have made custom hardware far more approachable. Expect to spend most of your time on shielding, grounding, and noise rather than on the interesting part.

The part most projects skip

Ethics, consent, and neurorights

If you're collecting neural data from anyone other than yourself, you have obligations, and if you plan to publish or work with an institution you'll likely need ethics board review. Even outside that, informed consent that a participant actually understands is the floor, not the ceiling.

Neural data is increasingly regulated as its own category. Several US states have moved to classify it as sensitive personal information, and UNESCO has adopted a global recommendation on neurotechnology ethics. Design your data handling for the rules arriving, not the rules of five years ago, because retrofitting consent is far harder than building it in.

Claim discipline Practice

The fastest way to lose credibility in this field is to describe a state correlate as a thought reader. Write the most conservative accurate description of what your system does, then market that. It's also the version that survives contact with a regulator.

Know when you've become a medical device Practice

Wellness framing has limits. The moment you claim to diagnose, treat, or mitigate a condition, you're in a different regulatory category in most jurisdictions. Find out where that line sits before you write the landing page.

Report your controls Practice

Sham conditions are hard in neurofeedback and that's exactly why they matter. If you can't blind participants, say so plainly and interpret accordingly. Honest limitations sections build more trust than clean results do.

Plan for the shutdown Practice

Decide now what happens to user data and device function if your company ends. Publishing that policy up front is a genuine competitive advantage in a market where buyers have been burned by bricked hardware.
